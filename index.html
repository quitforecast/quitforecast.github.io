See how the window is found

See it coming.

Cravings are not random, and yours are not a mystery. They arrive at hours, and hours can be counted.

Log a craving in two taps. Once enough of them have landed on enough separate days, the app names the two hours yours gather in. Not a mood, not a score: a count you could have made yourself, out of your own log.

Two things then happen, at two different distances. The dial turns to "Window soon" about three hours out, so a glance tells you what kind of evening is coming. The notification is separate and lands about 45 minutes ahead, close enough to be a prompt rather than a forecast, and it arrives with the play and not just the warning. This is the app's own line, unchanged: A walk, some water, or the SOS breath is enough to get through it.

In our own backtest, a pattern that shows up on most days cleared the bar on the third or fourth day of logging. One that only shows on Fridays took about a month, because it needs four or five Fridays before it can say anything about Fridays.

A clock face of your own day. Midnight at the top, amber over your window, a cream dot at right now.

When the hours arrive it says so, and every fact underneath is counted off the same log.

Real screens, from a real build, on a seeded demo log rather than a person's, one that happens to peak in the early evening. Yours will name different hours, or none at all. A day with two genuine patterns gets two arcs; almost nobody has two.

What it takes before it says anything

A window has to clear a statistical bar before the app will speak. The everyday reading is counted across your last thirty days; a pattern that only shows on one weekday is counted across your last eight of that weekday, so it takes longer to earn. The floor either way is four separate days holding a craving in the same two hours, and the reading before it has to have named those same two hours already. For some logs it never clears, and the app says nothing rather than invent one. The counter, the milestone timeline and the SOS tools work from the first minute.

The dial predicts nothing

A ring of your day, midnight at the top, the amber arc your window, the cream dot now. Under it, one word: Outside it, Window soon, In window, Held. It says where you are standing in your own pattern. There is no score in it, no colour scale, and no red.

Four reasons to believe your window.

  1. It is your data, not an average

    The window comes out of your log and nobody else’s. No population average is ever borrowed to fill a gap.

  2. It stays quiet when it does not know

    Below the bar, it says nothing. In our own backtest, across twelve simulated logs, 97 percent of the warnings it gave were for hours a craving really did arrive in, and for the person whose cravings had no pattern to find it stayed silent for good. Most apps guess and hope, because silence looks like a broken feature.

  3. Every claim names its source

    The physiology is cited on the screen that makes the claim, and the catalogue admits which moves have no study behind them.

  4. Nothing leaves your phone

    No accounts, no ads, no analytics, no third-party SDKs. Five things can leave, and each one is a thing you do on purpose. They are named in full further down.

Five habits, five different withdrawals.

Smoking, vaping, alcohol, cannabis, caffeine. Each written separately, because they are not the same withdrawal.

Nicotine leaves in days while the receptors take weeks, and it leaves smoking and vaping differently: smoking spends its first day clearing carbon monoxide that vaping never put there. Alcohol withdrawal can be a medical emergency, so that timeline opens with the warning signs and the number to call rather than a milestone. Cannabis takes sleep and appetite, and hands them back over the first ten days. Caffeine is a headache with a deadline. You can run more than one at a time, and each keeps its own clock, its own timeline and its own colour.

The app opens the alcohol path with a warning you have to acknowledge before it asks you anything else. The exact wording, and the five signs that mean 911, are at the foot of this page.

There is no streak to lose.

A counter that resets to zero has told you the last forty days did not happen. They happened.

A slip restarts the clock and nothing else. Your timeline keeps every run you have made, the savings tin never resets, and the lessons stay in the log where the next window can use them. Nobody is scored and nobody is scolded.

There is no improved you at the end of this. Only you, uninterrupted.

The app's own words, at the top of the Health tab.

Nothing is being added. Something is being removed. Nicotine did not add a self, and quitting does not build one. It only sets down a weight you had stopped noticing you carried, and what remains was always what you were.

The flatness of week one is not a personality. It is a withdrawal state, and it has a duration. QuitForecast names that duration hour by hour, from twenty minutes to fifteen years, with a separate timeline for smoking, vaping, alcohol, cannabis and caffeine.

Every hour of it, what to do about it, and the evidence for each one

Open a milestone and it holds what is healing, and what you might feel today. Through the acute window it holds a third thing: what actually helps, at that hour, not in general. That is 67 of the 109 stages, and they are the 67 that need it. Cannabis and caffeine carry a move at every stage they have, out to a year. Smoking, vaping and alcohol carry one at every hour through the first three days, then stop, because by the second week there is nothing left to get through.

Your body keeps the receipts.

The forecast says your heart calms and your breathing settles. Your own watch can check whether it did.

Connect Apple Health and five measures chart themselves against your own average from the fourteen days before you quit: resting heart rate, heart rate variability, cardio fitness, respiratory rate and blood oxygen. The dashed line is your before. The marker is the day you stopped. Every card says what published research expects and cites it, then shows what actually happened to you, which is not always the same thing and is never edited to agree.

All five come off a wearable. An Apple Watch writes them to Apple Health, usually overnight, so nothing appears the moment you connect and the first point can take until tomorrow morning. What research expects is written per habit, not in general: the HRV card says something different for cannabis than it does for smoking.

The app keeps no copy. Every visit to the Health tab reads Apple Health fresh and stores none of it, so there is no health series on your phone, none in the iCloud backup, and nothing to leak. It is read, shown to you, and let go.

A craving is a wave.

It crests and fades in a few minutes whether or not you fight it. The whole job is to still be there afterwards.

SOS times the wave while you ride it, and after a few rides it can tell you how long yours actually last, which is almost always shorter than they feel. The app is careful about this and so is the page: most cravings crest and pass within a few minutes, a stubborn one can run longer, and that is still normal. There is a breath pacer, a body scan, a grounding exercise, a passage to read, and two games for when thinking is not on the table.

Every one of these is free, permanently. So are the lines: 988 to call or text, the SAMHSA helpline, 1-800-QUIT-NOW, and 911, all of them dialable from the foot of this page.

The worst ten minutes of your week are not a sales moment.

The log is not homework.

It is where every hour on your dial came from, and it costs you two taps.

Two taps records a craving, from the phone or from your wrist. What comes back are facts in your own numbers, each one a thing you could have counted by hand:

  • Boredom and Stress often fire together, 31 times so far. Plan for the pair, not just each one.
  • Your cravings are spacing out: about every 8 hrs two weeks ago, about every 10 hrs now.
  • Your last 6 timed SOS rides ended in about 3 minutes.
  • Your log holds 164 cravings since you quit, and no slip at all.

Four sentences the engine renders, off the same seeded demo log as the screenshots, not a person's. Each appears only once the log holds enough to support it, and goes quiet again if it stops being true.

Nothing leaves.

Your habits and quit dates, your craving log, your slips, your savings, your settings and anything read from Apple Health live on your phone. There are no accounts and no passwords, because there is nothing to sign in to.

Five things can leave, and each one is a thing you do on purpose: an iCloud backup, into your own private database that we cannot read; a post to the community board, under an anonymous identifier that is never your name; a vote on somebody else's post, stored under that same identifier so a tap can be taken back; a report, if you flag a post for review; and a feedback message, if you write one. That is the whole list.

Reading the board sends nothing and needs no account. Opening it does ask iCloud who you are, so the board can show you which posts are yours and which ones you have already backed. Nothing is written unless you post, vote, report, or write to us.

Support.

Questions, bugs, or anything the app got wrong about you: write to info@quitforecast.com and a person will answer.

If you are in crisis, please do not wait on an email. In the US: call 988 for the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, or text 988 if talking is not on the table. 1-800-662-4357 is the SAMHSA National Helpline, free, confidential and open every day. 1-800-784-8669 is 1-800-QUIT-NOW, for the quit itself. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.

Outside the US, your local emergency number works the same way, and findahelpline.com lists crisis lines by country. That is the same link the app opens, and it is the only address on this page that points anywhere but here.

Alcohol withdrawal in particular can be dangerous, and this is the app's own wording, unchanged: Confusion, seeing or hearing things that are not there, fever, bad shaking, or a seizure means 911, immediately. If you drink heavily every day, a supervised taper is the safe route, so talk to a doctor before you stop.

QuitForecast is not a medical device and does not diagnose or treat anything. It holds no advice your own doctor should not overrule.
